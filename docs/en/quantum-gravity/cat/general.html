TL;DR — The essentials

  • The starting point is two observers' records. A falls in and B stays outside. They may never be able to bring their final records together. An observer is a physical system that receives and records signals; consciousness is not required.
  • CAT proposes an additional physical law. It supplements ordinary quantum correlations with irreversible gravitational selection of a “layer” and a subsequent change of routes. Layers are not another name for experimentally established parallel universes.
  • The concrete results have conditions. Specified finite models allow calculations of exact inward closure, two receipts from a common preparation, and recovery of quantum information.
  • Realization in nature remains unconfirmed. Actual stellar collapse, the endpoint of evaporation, a theory connecting all matrix sizes and clocks, and a gravitational experiment meeting the required conditions remain open tasks.

For the falling traveller, a route continues beyond the horizon. For someone watching from far away, there are exterior records that may carry the traveller's information. How far can these two descriptions be placed on a single map of spacetime?

Correlation Actualization Theory, or CAT, is a proposal in physics that begins with this question. It postulates that gravity can irreversibly select a record from quantum correlations, and that the received record can change how matter subsequently propagates. Version 4 connects this idea to matrix models, explicit calculations, and conditions for tests that could falsify particular predictions.

1. Two observers whose final records cannot meet

A takes a clock and a small detector into a large black hole. B remains in a distant laboratory. Here, an observer means a system that receives signals and leaves a physical record. Clocks, atoms, and detectors can carry records through changes in their states. A conscious experience of “seeing” does not activate a law of nature.

In general relativity, A can cross the horizon in a finite time on A's own clock. Light reaching B becomes increasingly delayed and redshifted. That does not mean B has observed A being destroyed at the horizon. The prediction of Hawking radiation is not, by itself, such an observation either. The difficulty is that A's final record cannot simply be brought back to the exterior and compared with B's.

CAT's working hypothesis allows an inward continuation for A while B's present physical description continues through a surface carrying A's correlations and through exterior processes. At that stage, B's description has no separate interior in which a second copy could reside. If B later chooses to fall, that produces a new sequence of receipts, rather than a joint comparison of the two earlier endpoints.

This motivation connects with black-hole complementarity, which already gives different observers different operational descriptions. CAT additionally seeks a concrete rule that can remove an inward physical route. B's lack of detailed information about A cannot, on its own, perform that additional physical job.

2. Making correlations and selecting a record

When an object interacts with a detector, their states become correlated. Information spreading into the environment can make quantum interference disappear from the part available to a laboratory. In an ordinary closed quantum system, completely reversing every change, including the environment and recoil, restores the initial state. The practical difficulty of doing this is separate from whether it is possible in principle.

CAT combines three stages. Ordinary interactions first create correlations. An actual gravitational receipt then irreversibly selects one of the distinguishable records with its Born probability. Finally, that record changes later propagation. As an additional law, selection removes the relative phase between different records: the information specifying how their quantum alternatives interfere.

The diagram separates ordinary reversible evolution, the selection added by CAT, and the alternative records that can result, using distinct boxes and branches. They do not represent parallel-universe plates discovered somewhere in space. Layers are defined through the correlations selected, the histories that follow, and the rules for comparing them at physical contacts. Merely drawing finer distinctions between layers does not create a new force. The account must specify what matter absorbs and what it records.

Reading the same apparatus's records in more detail or less detail must give the same total statistics when all outcomes are added together. CAT preserves this requirement. A difference between A's and B's continuations therefore requires more than a different way of summarizing information: it requires different physically received records and the different outputs those records permit.

3. What could change the available routes?

To formulate a theory mathematically, physicists use an “action” to specify how possible changes contribute. CAT compares mediating fields, direct interactions, and constraints at receiving contacts as ways for differences between layers to affect matter. Even if stationary objects experience the same force, that alone does not establish whether an independent wave carries it.

Similar effects can arise from different mechanisms
IdeaWhat it can doWhat remains undecided
A field carries an interactionChange another system's motion through emission, propagation, and absorptionWhether irreversible selection occurs or inward states disappear
Add finite dampingMake the transmission probability very smallWhether it becomes exactly zero
Restrict states at a receiving contactChange available routes according to received recordsWhether natural matter realizes the receipt and constraint

CAT's contact is a junction reached by the relevant material currents or signals. It is not an instantaneous remote-control switch. Absorption, recoil, transfer of a signed quantity, and its compensating destination must be included. Probabilities adding to one does not, by itself, establish conservation of energy or momentum.

Operations at independent locations must also satisfy conditions preventing a convenient change of order from sending a distant signal. Matching averages after discarding outcomes is insufficient: the retained pair of records and any later controlled operations must also agree. Deriving all these requirements in general quantum-gravitational settings remains open.

4. A difficult route and a route that does not exist

However dark an entrance becomes, a small nonzero transmission probability still leaves inward possibilities. Requiring ten binary comparisons to agree, for example, still permits an accidental complete match. Accumulating a finite amount of damping has the same limitation. CAT's target of exact closure requires removal of the inward-propagating states themselves.

Version 4 compares signed quantities received at two ends of a spherical port. Matching conditions leave one inward spatial link. A one-step mismatch in one direction leaves none; the opposite mismatch leaves two. Established mathematics on the sphere supplies the counting. The additional physical assumption is that matter propagation really obeys this constraint.

A combination of keys changing the number of usable passages is a useful analogy. One spatial link can still carry an entire quantum payload. The count concerns routes for propagation at the specified port, not the number of information bits. Whether the information passes inward or is transferred to a surface carrier requires its own analysis.

Extending the claim to a whole black hole requires closure of every relevant port, including additional particle species and alternative routes. Even some histories with an open route defeat exact global closure. There is also a causal restriction: a signal first received at a classical event horizon cannot then travel out from that same horizon along an ordinary outgoing light path. An exterior-radiation account must specify the surface's location and an available outward causal route.

5. From arrays of numbers to microscopic rules

The proposal's ordinary dynamics uses ten matrices and additional matter degrees of freedom. A matrix is an array of numbers; here matrices represent material configurations and interactions. CAT inherits interactions from the established IKKT matrix proposal and examines a correspondence with BFSS-type matrix dynamics when a clock is chosen.

The added prescription concerns a material configuration crossing a specified boundary while an excitation is actually absorbed. A signed quantity is transferred between a receiver and a compensating port, and the resulting record is selected. Merely crossing a mathematical boundary, or reversibly pumping a quantity, does not establish irreversible selection. An energy source and a physically realized transition are necessary.

Ten is the number of matrices, not the number of rows or columns in each one. CAT fixes no maximum matrix size. Its “nonperturbative” calculations treat the full specified interaction at a chosen size, clock, and port, rather than using only a weak-interaction expansion. This does not mean that a single physical theory connecting every matrix size has already been completed.

Comparisons with string theory, M theory, loop quantum gravity, causal sets, and causal dynamical triangulations help identify these boundaries. Similar counting or surface structures do not derive another theory's dynamics. Identifying a layer's integer label with M theory's eleventh direction would also require matching momentum, exchange amplitudes, and known force cancellations.

6. Can the required records arrive in time?

A rule for a passage does not explain infall if its key arrives too late. Starting from one collapsing material source, the account must follow signals to A and B, their absorption, and their ability to control the subsequent contact. Choosing convenient initial charges separately for A and B does not solve this preparation problem.

The available time also depends on the clock. In the paper's ten-solar-mass example, a specified segment of A's fall lasts 5.44 milliseconds on A's clock. After the calculated signal reaches a separate stationary receiver, that receiver has only about one microsecond on its clock before A arrives. These are different intervals and different clocks, illustrating why the receiving deadlines require separate calculations.

A large, heavy receiver does not guarantee adequate absorption. In a specified cold-molecule model, even a 70-kilogram receiver has an extremely small expected number of molecules excited by one pulse. Models prepared for collective response can show larger effects, but collective motion, timely signal arrival, and an actually compensated receipt remain three distinct requirements.

The star's interior also matters. Inside the homogeneous-dust model considered in the paper, a particular tidal component vanishes, leaving a receiver sensitive only to that component unexcited. Matter responding to another kind of curvature would require a different mechanism. Such negative examples help identify what natural collapse would have to prepare.

7. One preparation, two receipts, and quantum recovery

Version 4 gives a concrete apparatus that isolates a smaller part of the problem. One source between two receiving stations emits two successive pairs of pulses. Each station has two ends to compare, and the polarization determines which ends can absorb and which let a packet pass. Counting everything from the same preparation determines how A's and B's outcomes occur together.

In one specified setting, the source's two components have weights of 75% and 25%. A 64% absorption probability for a receptive pulse then gives B a closed route in 53.76% of trials. Positive confirmation of absorption at both A ends coincides with B's closure in approximately 19.66% of trials. These numbers describe a prepared apparatus starting with empty records. They neither describe a star collapsing into a black hole nor give a universal closure probability for infall.

The next question is whether unknown quantum information survives. Reading a zero or a one is insufficient: their quantum superposition and entanglement with an external reference must also be preserved. A list of musical loudnesses, for example, cannot reconstruct how the sounds are combined. Similarly, reproducing radiation's correct brightness does not guarantee recovery of quantum information.

In the finite example, recovery is exact conditional on B's closed histories if the specified photon, graviton, and residual-core carriers are all accessible. Losing some carriers reduces performance. Recovery can also fail if the receiver distinguishes and records components of the very quantum state being protected. What is accessible from outside matters as much as what is emitted.

This is not a process that turns one unknown quantum state into two copies freely available to the same observer. Each description follows one input and tests the outputs available to that observer. Supplying the required emission amplitudes and carriers during actual evaporation, through the final emitted quantum, remains an open task.

8. Reading clocks and geometry from material relations

Usually, a time and a place are supplied before an object's motion is calculated. The paper also offers a different description. Two material modes exchange excitation one unit at a time, providing clock readings. The states of other matter are then considered at those readings. This attempts to describe the order of events without an externally prescribed coordinate time.

It does not create time without assumptions. A sector in which the clock advances in one direction, an initial state, and the matrix algebra are still inputs. A physical clock can reverse, split, or exhaust its readings. Combining all such clock choices remains unresolved.

Distances and spacetime geometry are also read from the propagation of material waves. Particular matrix states can reproduce local geometries corresponding to four-dimensional gravitational waves. This does not automatically give Einstein's equations with arbitrary matter, gravity universally shared by every species, or a stable vacuum.

Where A and B can use the same spacetime map, they compare clocks, light-travel times, polarizations, and directions. Agreement must persist when comparisons pass through several overlapping regions, rather than only between two selected measurements. Some light paths can have matching travel times while another direction reveals a discrepancy. A “shared map” is an approximation supported by comparable physical records.

9. Singularities, vacua, and spatial scales

What physics lies beyond a classical calculation heading toward a singularity? The paper includes rotating matrix configurations that do not shrink to zero and instead turn around smoothly. Yet matter passing through a smooth geometry can emerge in a different state. Some examples also have growing fluctuations, so smoothness and stability must be tested separately.

The existence of such an alternative solution does not calculate the probability that a real black hole enters it. Several futures agreeing with limited past observations are also distinct from a physical transition between different vacua. The paper does not conclude that the early universe was born from such a turnaround.

Here, a “vacuum” is more than empty space: it is a candidate background determining particle and geometric properties. In a restricted string-derived setting, imposing conditions and grouping equivalent configurations leaves 115 candidates. A calculation of small changes around these candidates fixes the examined shape and coupling variables in 49 cases. These numbers do not count all CAT universes, nor establish 49 stable quantum vacua.

What fixes a common background scale, such as an internal-space radius, is another question. A rule comparing only relative differences can leave freedom to enlarge the entire configuration uniformly. In one specified static setting, ordinary quantum corrections cancel, while the free excitations examined all push the scale in the same direction and provide no restoring force. Whether a different bound state or CAT receipt helps can be decided only by calculating its actual coupling.

Many observers can inhabit one vacuum. Observer counts, counts of matrix solutions, and counts of inequivalent vacua are different. The mathematics of four-dimensional smooth structures discussed in the paper likewise does not prove that every observer has a separate universe.

10. What can stars and cosmological data test?

We cannot reverse the whole sky. Nevertheless, interference, polarization, oscillations, and distance relations can probe which records might have been lost. The essential steps are to specify the low-energy conditions being tested and distinguish effects that ordinary absorption or damping can also produce.

The paper also identifies incompatible candidates. Extending a particular homogeneous matrix cosmology across a supernova sample conflicts with the inferred expansion. A separate condition of exact coasting with a fixed acoustic ruler fails a two-point diagnostic using DESI data. The latter diagnostic was selected after seeing the data; it is neither a global statistical comparison of cosmological models nor an exclusion of late asymptotic coasting.

Extra forces on matter are tightly constrained too. A branch with a particular light field predicts too large a difference between titanium and platinum free fall to agree with MICROSCOPE. Calling a component dark matter or dark energy also requires its pressure and radial force, not merely its density, to match observations. A small change in a fitted Hubble constant does not identify CAT's irreversible selection.

11. Closing a route and testing a return

Inward closure asks whether a state can propagate beyond a particular port. A reversal experiment asks whether the initial quantum state is restored after the apparatus's ordinary changes are reversed. These are different quantities, even when both discussions use the language of returning. Closing an entrance does not contradict reading a state at an experiment's output.

For a pure initial state, an ideal complete reversal of ordinary quantum evolution returns to that state with 100% probability. If CAT selection distinguishes two equally weighted records, the probability becomes 50% under the paper's ideal conditions. This is a test of restoring the initial quantum superposition, not a test of walking back to the same location.

The design must also guard against a detector that always reports “returned.” Readout is calibrated on both returned and nonreturned reference states, while absorption is checked in separate trials. Directly measuring the record during the signal trial would itself create ordinary measurement decoherence. Environmental leakage and imperfect reversal must also be independently bounded across every input admitted by the design.

Under version 4's specified calibrations and error bounds, the proposed test gives an approximately 66% upper bound for CAT return and a 97% lower bound for ordinary reversal. These are qualified design bounds, not measurements. Trials failing calibration support no conclusion, and a gravitational apparatus producing and certifying the required absorption has not yet been realized.

An astrophysical gravitational-wave echo is a further, separate question. Zero inward propagation does not determine the outgoing wave's amplitude. Likewise, in the time-traveller thought experiment, a clock can show an old reading while a retained memory identifies a later visit. CAT's record argument does not establish a general classification of allowed or forbidden closed timelike curves.

12. What calculations check, and what nature must answer

The version-4 materials include the paper and supplement, numerical code, data provenance, calculation records, and Lean proof-assistant files. Lean checks whether mathematical conclusions follow from explicit hypotheses. It is not an instrument for observing nature directly.

13. The next answer must come from nature

The central next task starts with one collapsing material state and connects the signals reaching A and B from their pasts, timely receipts, closure of all B's relevant inward routes, and decoding from B's accessible radiation and residual core. This requires following one physical process through, beyond assembling separate favorable examples.

A completion must also connect different matrix sizes and clocks, recover our particles and four-dimensional gravity in a stable background, and explain the physical scales and constants. The endpoint of black-hole evaporation remains unresolved. Progress on these requirements is what would establish how far a proposal for a theory of everything has been realized.

Version 4 offers a way to make the question of who physically receives what, and how that affects future routes, calculable and testable. Connecting the mechanisms seen in finite models to natural collapse and to measurements that distinguish them from ordinary quantum processes is the next stage of the research.

Reference appendix: The tests, one by one

This catalogue connects the main discussion to all eleven local tests and all twelve astronomical and cosmological tests in the supplement. Each row examines a specified branch under its own conditions. They are not predictions that all apply simultaneously to one material or celestial object.

Eleven tests with controlled apparatus

These tests share a requirement: calibrate charge distinction and absorption on separate trials, and prepare the ordinary inverse including the environment, source, and recoil on the signal trials. If an ordinary measurement reads the record during a signal trial and thereby destroys interference, that alone does not establish CAT's distinctive selection law.

Local tests: what changes, and what the comparison distinguishes
TestComparison and necessary conditions
1. A tidally driven oscillatorWhen a prepared receiver distinguishes equally weighted empty and nonempty excitation, ideal return is one half, so complete return would contradict that receipt hypothesis. Matched electromagnetic driving and a gravitational pulse that misses the receiving boundary should both produce no excess loss.
2. The duration of a resonant pulseIn the specified resonance, pulse lengths of one half, one, and twice the reference duration give ideal returns of 75%, 50%, and 100%. Reverse the source motion and use an off-resonance control to calibrate the ordinary transition, then test for the central dip.
3. The spin axis recorded in mid-operationChange the intermediate recording axis or the number of independent records, and compare survival or inversion probabilities after undoing the rotation. If a confirmed, fully distinguishing receipt on an orthogonal axis still allows complete return after restoring the source and recoil, that irreversible-selection branch is excluded.
4. Recording two excitations together or separatelyThe same two excitations permit full entanglement recovery when only their common total charge is recorded, but independent distinction of their alternatives reduces the recovery fidelity to one half. Establish what was physically distinguished, separating the strength of excitation from the resolution of its record.
5. Grouping four receiversWith four independent, balanced receivers, recording whether any responded, the total number, or each individual response gives different return rates. Independently calibrating the four responses and the physical groupings allows identical high return across the three cases to test the branches associated with those distinctions.
6. Neutralizing the charge laterNeutralizing a balanced charge can leave return to the original state at 50%; another example with one-quarter exchange gives 75% neutrality but 31.25% return. Compare charge and phase in matched ensembles: neutrality alone is not evidence of quantum-state recovery.
7. The overlap of two bosonsWhen a receiver distinguishes three occupation outcomes, varying the overlap of two identical particles produces a minimum return of one third in the specified preparation. Calibrate overlap and charge to test the change; a simulation with quanta of material vibration checks the mathematics, while actual gravitational absorption remains a separate requirement.
8. Absorption that depends on spin and polarizationUnequal absorption limits the selected two-component code's optimal recovery fidelity to about 93.07%, while a one-sided receiver records only the absorbable sign. Reverse the receiver and control stray angular momentum: fidelity above the cap or equal responses for both signs would contradict the respective branches.
9. A four-level reference systemFor an equally mixed four-level input, records that narrow the input to a single quantum direction with total weight at least 80% limit recovery of entanglement with a reference to 40% fidelity. A different binary receiver has a cap near 98.1%, so the full input response and resolution must be reconstructed rather than inferred from the number of outcome labels.
10. Waiting without a receiptA certified interval without an occupied crossing produces no excess CAT loss, distinguishing it from a model that loses phase continuously while waiting; resolving three equally weighted charges instead gives ideal return of one third. Control vibrations, collisions, and ordinary radiation before interpreting time-dependent loss or complete return after distinguishing records.
11. A contact's charge difference and timingMatching charges leave one inward route at the specified port, while a negative unit mismatch leaves none, so transmission in a certified closed sector contradicts that constraint. Establish both end records within one history and test that the route cannot change in response to a new charge before its information arrives.

Twelve tests through observations of the sky

Astronomical systems cannot be inverted in full. Instead, estimate ordinary motion and damping independently and examine the surviving phase, polarization, or inferred distance. Without an independent constraint on the receipt fraction or surface reflectivity, an absence of observed loss cannot exclude every CAT branch.

Astronomical and cosmological tests: observations and their scope
SystemComparison and necessary conditions
1. Gravitational-wave echoes after mergerFor the nonrotating, thirty-solar-mass example, a rapidly reflecting surface 0.01% outside the horizon radius gives an initial round-trip delay of about 5.33 milliseconds. An observational limit below the echo required by a reflector with specified spin, propagation phase, reflectivity, and redshift excludes that reflector; inward closure alone determines no reflected amplitude.
2. Tides in an eccentric stellar binaryWhen the same material mode receives two opposite drives, its residual energy and phase distinguish common records, records of occupation parity, and records of the full number. A directly driven luminosity harmonic at twice the frequency does not by itself measure the required second-order material response.
3. Tides repeated on each orbitIn the specified resonant example retaining 90% of the amplitude each orbit, coherent energy grows to one hundred times the single-drive value, versus about 5.263 times when phase is erased each orbit. Independently measured detuning and damping allow a sharp resonance above the latter bound to exclude complete erasure on every passage.
4. Inspiral tides and gravitational-wave phaseEnergy lost from the orbit to tides changes gravitational-wave phase, allowing the loss required by a receipt model to be tested as a waveform shift. If the permitted shift remains below the required value after accounting for stellar matter properties and ordinary tidal dissipation, the specified branch fails.
5. Magnetar X-ray polarizationA receipt fully distinguishing left and right circular polarization weakens linear polarization already present, so high surviving polarization constrains the fraction of such receipts. Compare receipts after the last polarization-generating process, controlling energy band, stellar rotation phase, last scattering, and subsequent propagation.
6. A gravitationally lensed radio burstFor two images with a four-to-one intensity ratio and complete path distinction on half the trials, interference visibility is at most 0.4. Test whether stronger interference survives after accounting for receiver bandwidth, plasma, source size, and ordinary coherence.
7. Pulsars within a light-scalar haloComplete local records of particle number in the assumed light field remove phase shared by pairs between locations, while parity-only records can retain it. Independent evidence for the field is required before comparing phase-referenced pulsar timing variations, with the common Earth contribution and distance uncertainties controlled.
8. Supernova neutrinosComplete distinction of the two Earth-crossing components removes the energy-dependent interference pattern in the specified two-component model; partial distinction weakens it by the corresponding fraction. The comparison requires a sufficiently nearby supernova, contrasting source components, the Earth-matter effect, and adequate energy resolution.
9. Acoustic phase in the cosmic backgroundFor primordial quantum pairs, a complete particle-number record removes the specified phase correlation, whereas a record of the pair's net momentum permits it. Identify temperature and polarization signals that require this correlation after subsequent transport; the existence of acoustic peaks alone does not settle the question.
10. Expansion in three nearby directionsThe tested homogeneous spherical matrix state has two principal expansion rates of opposite signs, so compatible observations of expansion along all three directions exclude extending that state across the sample. Sample geometry, the low-redshift approximation, and peculiar velocities matter, and the same test applies to ordinary IKKT with that geometry.
11. Expansion history and changing redshiftIf cosmic size grows exactly in proportion to time and the acoustic ruler is constant, a particular distance combination remains constant and the redshift of the same object has zero time drift. Conflicting distances or drift test that branch over the specified interval, but the paper's two selected observational bins are a diagnostic chosen after inspection and do not exclude coasting approached in the distant future.
12. Distances measured with light and gravitational wavesComparing commonly calibrated standard candles, gravitational-wave standard sirens, and angular rulers tests species-dependent loss of forward propagation; losing 10% of photon intensity alone increases the luminosity-inferred distance by about 5.41%. Weaker individual signals and whole events missing from a sample require different statistical models of observational selection.
